How to read this

The gain is each family's utility under deferred acceptance minus its utility under the distance rule, in kilometre-equivalents, averaged over lottery draws. A family gains when the rule that reads its list places it somewhere it prefers to the school proximity would have given it. Positive numbers are the value of being asked.

The socioeconomic axis is the mean years of schooling of adults in the census blocks around a family's home. It is an ecological average, not a household measure: it says what kind of neighbourhood a family lives in, not how educated that family is. The paper is explicit that this supports a claim about the direction of incidence, less-advantaged families gain more, and not about its magnitude.

Why this model's line is flat, and the paper's is not. Every synthetic family's tastes and list length are drawn from the same estimated distribution, independently of where it lives. The only route by which status can touch the gain here is geography: which schools happen to be nearby, and how crowded they are. That route on its own produces no measurable gradient. The paper's gradient is estimated on real families, so it says that families in less advantaged neighbourhoods gain more than geography alone would predict, which is exactly the part a status-blind model cannot invent. Read the flat line as a statement about this model, not about Manta.

Two further limits. Synthetic families inherit the value of the 300 metre cell they were drawn from, so every family in a cell shares one socioeconomic value and the chart shows no within-neighbourhood variation. And the entry grades are where a gradient would mean something: in the congested Primary 1 the aggregate gain is near zero, the paper finds the gradient there indistinguishable from zero, and it scopes the distributional reading to the two preschool grades.
