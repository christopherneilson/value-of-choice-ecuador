What this means for the welfare comparison

Short lists are not a failure to find alternatives. Most single-school families say no acceptable public school is close enough, or that they are sure of a seat; only about 6% cite difficulty finding schools. That is what lets the paper read short lists as scarcity of acceptable nearby options rather than ignorance, and treat the estimated welfare gain as a lower bound on the value of acting on preferences.

But knowledge of the schools families did not list is thin: they know only 8% of them well. The demand model reads an omitted school as less wanted than any listed one, and some omissions are surely a matter of awareness rather than preference. The paper says so plainly; the simulator's awareness slider shows what happens to the comparison when families consider more or fewer of the schools around them.

Beliefs were conservative and the process was well rated, which is consistent with a system acting on preferences families already held. It is not an independent measure of the welfare gain: the survey was fielded under deferred acceptance only, so there is no distance-rule counterpart to compare it with.
