Try: switch the rule

Go from the distance rule to deferred acceptance. The share of families placed in a school they asked for jumps; the share placed in their first choice jumps more. Then switch to the benchmark and notice how little changes — that gap is the value of the algorithm, and it is small.

Try: tighten the seats

Drag seats below ×1 — or jump to seats ×0.6. As congestion rises, the first-choice share under deferred acceptance falls and the gain over the distance rule shrinks. This is why Primary 1, the most congested grade, gains least in the paper.

Try: make families alike

Turn idiosyncratic taste σε down to ×0.2: most families now want their nearest school, the distance rule nearly matches deferred acceptance, and the gain from choice shrinks from about 0.5 to 0.1 km per family. Turn it up to ×3 and the gain triples. Now try σξ instead — making every school equally desirable changes almost nothing, because families compare only a few nearby schools. The value of choice lives in what each family wants, not in a ranking of schools a planner could observe — the paper's imagery result, in one slider.

Try: follow one family

Click any dot, or open family 16, which lives 170 m from a school but wants one 590 m away: the distance rule sends it to its fourth choice, deferred acceptance to its first. Each family's card shows its list, the school next door, and where each rule sends it — with the shortfall from its first choice in km-equivalent. Then make families know only their nearest school and watch nearly everyone ask for it, or let every family know every school and watch the value of choice grow as lists reach further.

Families are synthetic: drawn from the paper's estimated demand model over the real schools, with homes sampled from a coarse density grid — no real applicant appears. School desirability is shown as a quintile band only. Rules follow the deployed design: deferred acceptance runs on the reported list with the remaining schools appended in distance order (priorities: sibling, reported, appended; one lottery per applicant–school pair); the distance rule assigns in distance order with one lottery per applicant; the benchmark applies stable improvement cycles to deferred acceptance (paper, Proposition 2).
