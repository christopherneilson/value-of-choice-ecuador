How to read this

What was calibrated, and what was not. Every parameter of the model is the paper's published estimate; school desirability is the band the paper's estimates put each school in; homes are drawn inside a 300 m density grid of applicant homes with cells of fewer than five households removed. One thing was tuned: how many nearby schools a family compares before writing its list, one plus a Poisson draw with mean 1.5, about two and a half schools. Drawn over all 55 schools instead, the model would put the nearest school first for only about 40% of families against 65% observed, and no rescaling of the taste parameters fixes that without breaking the welfare numbers. Small consideration sets are also what the paper's survey says families actually know.

What matches. The access story: the share placed in a listed school and in the first choice under each rule, by grade and pooled, and the way both fall as the market tightens from the entry grade to Primary 1. Distances to the nearest school and the share who rank it first in the two later grades.

What does not. Three gaps are the price of the small consideration sets. Families who choose a school other than their nearest do not go as far out of their way as real ones (about 0.25 km against 0.6), so the welfare gain in kilometres is about 70% of the paper's; in the entry grade the nearest-first share is 58% against 65%. And Primary 1's "share of the range recovered" is not comparable at all: in the paper that ratio is near-undefined for the congested grade (its confidence interval spans −77% to +36%), while in the synthetic world, where preferences are complete, it is well defined. The congestion story lives in the first-choice share, which matches.

Why it matters. Nothing on this site is evidence about Manta beyond what the paper reports; the synthetic market is a device for seeing how the paper's conclusions depend on its ingredients. Where a slider moves a number here, the paper's estimates say by roughly how much it would move there.
