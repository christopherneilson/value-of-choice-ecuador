The Value of Choice in Centralized School Assignment · the argument, step by step

Why choice matters — even when most families want the school next door

The setting

One city, one entry grade, one question

In 2021 Manta replaced one centralized assignment rule with another. Before, a coordinated algorithm sent each child to the nearest public school with a seat. After, the same kind of algorithm — deferred acceptance — placed children according to the ranked lists their families submitted.

Coordination did not change. Digitization did not change. What changed was the objective: proximity, or preferences. That makes Manta a rare place to ask what acting on families' preferences is worth on its own.

Step 1

Most families want the nearest school. A quarter don't.

Ask families to rank schools and most put the one next door first. That is why a distance rule looks nearly optimal. But more than a quarter rank a school that is not their nearest, and they are willing to travel for it — a median of 0.6 km further in the real data.

A distance rule cannot see this. It treats every extra metre as pure cost.

Step 2

What the distance rule does with that

Under the old rule a family's list plays no role. The families coloured on the map are placed where proximity puts them: those who wanted the school next door are fine; those who wanted somewhere else are sent to the nearest school anyway, and the seat they wanted may go to someone who did not.

In the real data only 42% of applicants got their first choice, and only half were placed in any school they had listed.

Step 3

Act on preferences instead

Same families, same schools, same seats, same lotteries. Deferred acceptance simply takes the lists seriously: each family proposes to its first choice, schools hold the applicants with the best priority, rejected families move down their list.

The share in their first choice jumps, the share in any listed school jumps, and travel rises by only a few hundred metres — because most families were asking for something nearby all along.

Step 4

Does a cleverer algorithm help? Barely.

Deferred acceptance is not the most efficient mechanism imaginable. Trades that respect priorities can still improve on it — the paper's constrained-efficient benchmark finds them all. Switch to it and almost nothing moves.

That is the paper's central finding: the value comes from eliciting preferences, not from the choice of algorithm. In New York City's move to coordinated assignment the algorithmic refinements were worth 3% of the welfare range; in Manta, 0.3%.

Step 5

What does matter: seats

Preferences can only be honoured where there are seats to honour them with. Shrink the seats and watch deferred acceptance lose its advantage: fewer families in their first choice, and a smaller share of the possible gain captured.

Manta's three grades sit at different points on this curve. In the entry grade deferred acceptance places 93% of families in their first choice; in the congested Primary 1 grade, 33%. Congestion, not the algorithm, is what limits the value of choice.

Step 6

Who gains

The gains are progressive. Sorting neighbourhoods by schooling, the paper finds mean gains of +0.79 km-equivalent in the lowest quartile against +0.59 in the highest in Preschool 1 (+0.49 against +0.15 in Preschool 2). The nearest school a poorer neighbourhood offers is drawn from a thinner menu, so looking past proximity helps those families most.

The socioeconomic proxy is a block average, so the paper reads this as evidence on the direction of incidence, not a precise ranking.

Step 7

Could a planner have guessed?

If what families want were mostly school quality, a well-informed planner could rank schools and assign accordingly, no lists needed. The paper measured schools from satellite and street-level imagery — footprint, condition, greenery, and more — and found that most of what makes a school desirable to a given family stays unexplained.

The model says the same thing from the other side: shrink families' idiosyncratic tastes and the value of choice collapses; make schools identical and almost nothing changes. Choice is worth something because families differ, in ways a planner cannot observe.
