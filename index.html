The Value of Choice in Centralized School Assignment

Welfare Gains from Acting on Family Preferences in Ecuador

Gregory Elacqua (IDB) · Isabel Jacas (ConsiliumBots) · Thomas Krussig (UC Berkeley) · Carolina Méndez (IDB) · Christopher A. Neilson (Yale)

JEL: C78, D47, I20, I21 · Keywords: mechanism design; centralized student assignment; school choice; Ecuador

We study a 2021 reform in Manta, Ecuador, that replaced one centralized assignment rule with another: a rule minimizing home-to-school distance gave way to deferred acceptance acting on families' reported rank-ordered preferences, which the mechanism's incentive properties give them no reason to misstate. Because a single coordinated algorithm cleared the market both before and after, what principally changed was the objective, isolating the value of acting on preferences while holding coordination fixed—the counterfactual for the many systems that assign by proximity. A distance rule looks nearly optimal—most families rank their nearest school first—yet more than a quarter do not. Acting on their preferences raises the share of applicants placed in a listed school from 50% to 78%, and in their first choice from 42% to 70%, at a cost of 0.32 km of additional travel. Estimating preferences from the submitted rankings, we find that deferred acceptance closes about 99.6% of the distance between the distance rule and the constrained-efficient benchmark in the entry grade, while further efficiency-enhancing mechanisms add almost nothing: there, the gains come from eliciting preferences, not from the choice of algorithm. They are also progressive, and they echo the evidence from New York City's move to coordinated assignment in a very different setting.
