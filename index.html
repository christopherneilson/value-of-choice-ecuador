The Value of Choice in Centralized School Assignment

Welfare Gains from Acting on Family Preferences in Ecuador

Gregory Elacqua (IDB) · Isabel Jacas (ConsiliumBots) · Thomas Krussig (UC Berkeley) · Carolina Méndez (IDB) · Christopher A. Neilson (Yale)

JEL: C78, D47, I20, I21 · Keywords: mechanism design; centralized student assignment; school choice; Ecuador

Apply yourself →

We study a 2021 reform in Manta, Ecuador, that replaced one centralized assignment rule with another: a rule minimizing home-to-school distance gave way to deferred acceptance acting on families' reported rank-ordered preferences, which the mechanism's incentive properties give them no reason to misstate. Because a single coordinated algorithm cleared the market both before and after, what principally changed was the objective, isolating the value of acting on preferences while holding coordination fixed—the counterfactual for the many systems that assign by proximity. A distance rule looks nearly optimal—most families rank their nearest school first—yet more than a quarter do not. Acting on their preferences raises the share of applicants placed in a listed school from 50% to 78%, and in their first choice from 42% to 70%, at a cost of 0.32 km of additional travel. Estimating preferences from the submitted rankings, we find that deferred acceptance closes about 99.6% of the distance between the distance rule and the constrained-efficient benchmark in the entry grade, while further efficiency-enhancing mechanisms add almost nothing: there, the gains come from eliciting preferences, not from the choice of algorithm. They are also progressive, and they echo the evidence from New York City's move to coordinated assignment in a very different setting.

Interactive

APPLY

Apply to school in Manta yourself →

Put your home on the map, rank the schools around you and hand in the list. You compete for the same seats as the synthetic families, and see what each rule gives you, how much the lottery moves it, and why lying does not pay.
STORY

Why choice matters, step by step →

A guided walk through the argument with a live model beside it: most families want the school next door, a quarter don't, what each rule does with that, why the algorithm barely matters and seats do, who gains, and why a planner could not have guessed.
WHO GAINS

Who gains? →

The gain from acting on preferences, family by family: a map of winners and losers, and the gain against the schooling of each family's neighbourhood. The incidence matches the paper; the progressive gradient does not, and why is the interesting part.
SIMULATOR

Choose the rule →

Manta's real schools and a synthetic population of families drawn from the paper's estimated model. Switch between the distance rule, deferred acceptance and the constrained-efficient benchmark, tighten the seats, and change how families' tastes are distributed — the paper's comparison, live in your browser.
TOY MARKET

Does lying pay? →

Three families, three schools, one seat each. Hand in a ranking under the Boston mechanism and under deferred acceptance and see when misreporting helps — and why, under the rule Manta adopted, it never can.
GAME

Could a planner have guessed? →

A guessing game on real schools: from ten imagery-derived attributes, guess how desirable families found each school, then compare your score with a statistical planner. Most of desirability is not in the attributes.
SURVEY

What families said →

The pre-results parent survey as aggregates you can slice: why lists were short, how well families knew the schools they did not list, how confident they were and whether they were right. No microdata.
FIGURE

Two welfare ladders →

The paper's central figure, live: New York's move to coordinated assignment beside Manta's move from a distance rule to preferences, on a common scale — click each band to see what it can and cannot attribute.
